About

A university-run week inside a global festival.

Qiskit Fall Fest is an annual season of student-run quantum computing events on campuses around the world, supported by IBM Quantum. This is the BITS Pilani, Pilani Campus edition.

The festival

What is Qiskit Fall Fest?

Every autumn, student organisers at universities around the world run their own quantum computing events, with support from IBM Quantum: workshops, lectures and hackathons built on shared teaching materials and a shared identity.

There’s no single central event. Each campus designs its own programme, so ours is shaped around what BITS Pilani students want to learn and who’s here to teach it.

The host

Why Pilani

Quantum computing sits where physics, electronics and computer science meet, and so does our campus. The week is taught by students with hands-on Qiskit experience, alongside BITS faculty and guest speakers.

It’s written for undergraduates who are new to quantum. We start from the prerequisites, so you don’t need a physics background. Basic Python helps, and that’s all you need.

What the week covers

Six threads, in order

  • FoundationsThe toolkit, minus the intimidation: Linear algebra, probability and statistics, and the core ideas of quantum mechanics, taught from scratch, with Qiskit set up and ready to go.
  • QubitsMeet the qubit: Superposition, measurement and single-qubit gates, then entanglement through Bell states, with your first circuits built in Qiskit.
  • Circuits & algorithmsFrom gates to algorithms: Build multi-qubit circuits gate by gate, then see how interference delivers quantum speedups in Grover's search and other core algorithms.
  • Variational & QMLWhere quantum meets machine learning: Train variational quantum circuits, learn how data is encoded into qubits, then build and test quantum classifiers in a hands-on QML workshop.
  • CryptographyFrom RSA to quantum-safe: Understand classical cryptography and why quantum computers threaten it, then implement BB84 quantum key distribution yourself and watch it catch an eavesdropper.
  • PhotonicsComputing with light: See how photons carry qubits, how beam splitters and interferometers perform quantum operations, and where photonic hardware and quantum networks are headed.

    Track 1 · Problem statement

    Isogenies on Oasis

    How Ankan sees campus 1 · The password 2 · The phone on the Clock Tower 3 · The Oasis map 4 · The Djinns Details

    With Oasis coming up in a few days, our beloved physics assoc members, Shashank, Gaurav, Shreyash, Aditya and Soham, were looking forward to having some fun. But suddenly, they get a call from Ankan Pal telling them to meet him in his chambers for a “fun game”. The members, knowing that the “fun” was not going to be very fun, hesitantly went to meet him.

    Upon meeting him, they see him excited and a bit concerned as well. “We have a problem at hand, and if you guys want a Domino’s treat from me, you are going to need to help me a bit.”

    Not particularly interested in Domino’s, but liking Ankan Pal and being curious students, our protagonists decided to hear it out.

    Ankan explains. He suspects that with Oasis coming up, Djinns have started showing up on campus. He has MFA set up on his Google account, and last night one of them wiped the password clean out of his head and ran off with his phone. He has a crypto test to make and needs to log in to do it. All he remembers is that he took inspiration for the password from the routes between the Maths department and ANC. And when he used Find My Device, it showed his phone on top of the Clock Tower, which is exactly where you’d leave a phone if you could fly.

    Our protagonists decide they want more than a Domino’s treat, and that their reward should be proportional to how hard it is to get to the top of the Clock Tower. Ankan, a bit too quickly, agrees.

    Also, it turns out that with Djinns wandering around and so many people running so many events all over campus during Oasis, Ankan Pal was tasked with making a map, and he is too lazy to do it himself. So, multiple birds with one step.

    How Ankan sees campus

    Ankan, of course, doesn’t see campus the way normal people do. He goes to the board.

    “Every spot on campus is a curve,” he says.

    EA : y2 = x3 + Ax2 + x,  with A ∈ 𝔽 = 𝔽p(i),  p ≡ 3 (mod 4),  i2 = −1

    “From every spot there are three roads, which are 2-isogenies. You came in on one, so there are two ways forward, 0 and 1. To know which is which, take the two 2-torsion points other than (0, 0) and sort their x-coordinates by key(u + v·i) = u + p·v to get r0 and r1. Bit b means you take the road through rb. You land on a curve you can write as EA′ or E−A′ (the two forms where (0, 0) is the road back to where you came from, i.e., it generates the kernel of the dual isogeny); take whichever has the smaller key. A password is just a string of bits, so it’s a route, and where you end up is its j-invariant. Call that H(route), and call the length of the route L.” (Every spot has A2 ≠ 4, so the curve is never singular.)

    Part 1

    The password

    Ankan’s password is one of the 12-step routes from the Maths department to ANC, but he doesn’t remember which one, so our protagonists need to find all of them. And since he’s going to sit there typing them in one by one, he wants to be sure the list is complete, and he wants them to prove it to him with a quantum procedure and tell him how confident they are. He also wants to know how many queries it took (one query is one use of the one-step circuit), compared with what a classical search and the best possible quantum search would need, in terms of N = 2L and the number of routes k.

    Aditya points out that it’s only 4096 routes and they could just walk all of them. Ankan laughs a bit. That works on this campus, sure, but he only drew them a small one. The real campus, the one his test is about, has p around 2256 and something like 2254 spots, so they have to do it the way they’d have to do it there.

    So the rules are as follows:

    • They’re allowed to walk every road once before they know where they’re going, which is at most two moves per spot, and turn that into a quantum circuit that takes one step coherently. This survey can’t depend on A0, L or j*.
    • After that, no planning routes on paper. That means no counting paths, no going backwards from ANC, no meet-in-the-middle, and no listing routes. The only classical thing allowed is walking a route they measured, once, to check it.
    • They can’t build an oracle out of evaluations of H either. The “are we at ANC?” check has to look at the spot itself, inside the circuit. On the small campus it can come from the survey, but on the real one it has to be arithmetic, which is what Part 3 is for.
    • And if it turns out no route actually gets to ANC, their code should say so instead of dying.

    Part 2

    The phone on the Clock Tower

    On Ankan’s campus, the top of the Clock Tower is the set of all curves whose j-invariant is in 𝔽p. On the small campus almost a third of routes end up there, so it’s not that impressive. On the real campus it’s more like one spot in √p, which is where it gets hard.

    Since our protagonists wanted their reward to be proportional to how hard it is to get up there, Ankan says fine: tell me how hard it is. Concretely:

    • What does it cost a quantum computer, as a power of p, to find a route of length Θ(log p) that ends on top of the Clock Tower?
    • They have to prove the upper bound, saying which theorems they borrowed.
    • They have to prove a matching lower bound in a model they define properly, and explain why nobody can prove one for the real arithmetic.

    He also wants data on how often routes end up there as p grows, with the exponent fitted. For the data they’re allowed to walk random routes classically, since that’s measuring the campus, not looking for the phone. And he wants to see them actually do it on a campus where at most 1 in 32 routes gets there, with success plotted against Grover iterations, next to the theory curve and next to just walking around classically.

    Part 3

    The Oasis map

    Now for the map Ankan was too lazy to make. The catch is that the Djinns don’t like being written down, and anything that looks like a table of roads just goes missing overnight. Plus, during Oasis the campus is huge and nobody is going to write down every road anyway. So the map can’t be a table this time. It has to work out every road by itself.

    For p = 1019, our protagonists have to build one step as a reversible circuit using only arithmetic in 𝔽. That means doing the square root, the key comparison and the sign choice, and cleaning up every ancilla afterwards. They check it on every spot and both bits. Then they tell Ankan what the same circuit costs at p ≈ 2256, including checking whether you’re on the Clock Tower, and what getting his phone back (Part 2) would cost there.

    Part 4

    The Djinns

    There’s one more thing bothering Ankan. The Djinns aren’t done. The night before the fest, they’re going to go around campus and swap every signboard according to some secret random shuffle that only they know. The roads stay the same; only the names change. After that, all anyone has is:

    • the name of the spot they start from;
    • a Djinn who, if asked, tells them the name behind road 0 or road 1;
    • a volunteer at every venue who just says yes or no to “is this the Clock Tower?” and “is this ANC?”.

    So he wants to know what exactly their speed-ups were depending on. What still works after the Djinns are done, what doesn’t, and why? Which classical tricks still work? And can they prove the Part 2 lower bound in a world run by Djinns?

    Shashank, Gaurav, Shreyash, Aditya and Soham look at each other, then at the Clock Tower, and decide the Domino’s had better be a large.

    Track 2 · Problem statement

    The Silent Group

    Break a three-party Diffie–Hellman exchange in a group you can never look at.

    Your task What you get Rules Scoring Submitting Getting started Notes on the physics FAQ
    github.com/ProAltro/qff-hackathon

    Three members of the Illuminati, known only as Galileo, Bernini and Milton, are planning a secret meeting. The time and place are never written down. Instead, the three of them agree on a shared secret K, and K is the meeting.

    They don’t trust paper, and they don’t trust ordinary arithmetic. Every key exchange the Order runs goes through the Silent Group. This is a sealed quantum device that holds one element of a finite abelian group in a quantum register. You hand it a control qubit and an instruction such as “multiply by h to the power k”. If the control is |1⟩, the device multiplies its register by hk. If the control is |0⟩, nothing happens. That’s all it does. It has no display and no readout, and it can’t be opened. Outside the Order, all anyone knows is that the group is finite and abelian, with at most 260 elements.

    To set a meeting, the three of them agree on a public element g. Galileo, Bernini and Milton each pick secret exponents a, b and c, and broadcast A = ga, B = gb and C = gc. Then they pass messages around the circle over private channels: Galileo sends Ba to Milton, Bernini sends Cb to Galileo, and Milton sends Ac to Bernini. Each raises what they receive to their own secret, and all three arrive at the same key, K = gabc.

    You’ve intercepted the public broadcasts g, A, B and C for three upcoming meetings. The private messages went over channels you couldn’t tap. Tonight, for a few hours, you also have the device itself. Find each meeting’s K.

    You have four things to keep in mind:

    • The device is old. It raises elements to powers by repeated squaring, so hk takes about log2 k internal operations. Every operation drains its power cell.
    • The device is noisy. Each multiplication leaves a small random error in the phase it kicks back. The bigger the power, the bigger the error.
    • The Order suspects a mole. Before some meetings, one or more members broadcast a forgery: an element that isn’t a power of g at all. No real key comes out of that exchange, and the meeting is a trap. Spot it.
    • The power cell is per meeting. Every shot of every circuit you run against a meeting’s broadcasts drains that meeting’s budget.

    Your task

    Each meeting gives you four element names: g, A, B and C. You can’t print group elements, so every answer is a number:

    FieldMeaning
    orderord(g): the smallest n > 0 with gn = e
    secretabc mod ord(g), so that K = gsecret
    secret = "none"if any of A, B or C is not a power of g (a forgery)

    Always report order, even for a forgery.

    What you get

    The repo is a Python package, silentgroup, that simulates the device on your laptop:

    NameWhat it does
    oracle_gate(element, power)A one-qubit Qiskit gate. Its qubit is the control, and it multiplies the device’s register by element ** power. power can be any int, including negative or very large values. The register itself is implicit: you never allocate, touch or measure it. It starts in the identity e.
    Oracle(instance).run(circuit, shots) returns counts. Keys are bitstrings with classical bit 0 rightmost. Also: .cost(circuit, shots), .budget_left(), .queries_used, .elements
    load_public("public_1")One of two practice meetings at full size. Their answers are in silentgroup/instances/.
    MockGroup(moduli, ...)A group ℤd1 × ℤd2 × … that you choose, for debugging. Its answer is known, and forged="C" (or "AB", …) makes those broadcasts forgeries.
     
    from qiskit import QuantumCircuit
from silentgroup import MockGroup, Oracle, load_public, oracle_gate

qc = QuantumCircuit(1, 1)
qc.h(0)
qc.append(oracle_gate("g", 5), [0])   # controlled multiplication by g^5
qc.h(0)
qc.measure(0, 0)

Oracle(MockGroup([2**20, 6], seed=1), noise=0).run(qc, shots=100)   # perfect device, for debugging
Oracle(load_public("public_1")).run(qc, shots=100)                   # realistic device

    The source is all there, and you’re welcome to read it.

    Rules

    1. Qubits. At most 16 qubits per circuit. The device’s register doesn’t count.
    2. Gates. Anything Qiskit can express on your own qubits: standard and library gates, measure, reset, and if_test conditioned on a classical bit or register. for_loop, while_loop and switch_case aren’t supported, so unroll loops in Python. Gates must be fully bound, with no free parameters.
    3. Cost. Each oracle_gate(h, k) costs max(1, bit_length(|k|)) queries per shot. Oracle gates in both branches of an if_test are charged. A run costs the circuit’s cost per shot times the number of shots.
    4. Noise. Each oracle call adds an independent Gaussian phase error to the phase it kicks back. Its standard deviation is 0.002 × √(that call’s cost) turns, where 1 turn = 2π radians.
    5. Budget. Each hidden meeting has 80,000 queries. Once they run out, the device stops responding.
    6. Time. Each hidden meeting gets 10 minutes of wall-clock time.
    7. Adaptivity. Your solver can look at the results of one run before building the next.
    8. Fair play. Your solver may interact with the device only through the oracle object. An attempt to inspect, import or attack the judge scores 0 for that meeting.

    • Find the full structure of the subgroup generated by g, A, B and C, as a product ℤn1 × ℤn2 × ….
    • Derive a query-cost bound for your method and compare it with what you measure.
    • Characterise how your solver degrades as the noise grows.
    • Solve with as few qubits as possible.

    Notes on the physics

    The register starts in the identity state |e⟩, and the device never measures it. G is abelian, so all the multiplication operators Uh share one eigenbasis. There is one eigenvector |χ̂⟩ for each character χ of G, with

    Uh |χ̂⟩ = e2πi χ(h) |χ̂⟩,

    and |e⟩ is the uniform superposition of all these eigenvectors. Work out what that means for what your measurements can tell you. The simulator in silentgroup/simulator.py does exactly this calculation.

    FAQ

    Can I see the group?
    Only for MockGroup and the two public meetings. The hidden groups exist only on the judge’s machine.
    Is the hidden group cyclic? How big is it?
    Assume nothing beyond “finite, abelian, at most 260 elements”. Hidden groups vary in shape.
    Why is my answer different each run?
    Every shot is random, and so is the noise. Design for that.
    Do failed runs cost queries?
    A run that’s refused for breaking the rules (too many qubits, an unknown element, an unsupported gate) costs nothing. A run that executes costs its full price, whatever you do with the result.
